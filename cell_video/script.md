# Inside the Cell: narration script (draft v2, fact-checked)

Target: ~7.5 min at ~150 words/min, 16:9, English.
[Brackets] = what is on screen.

---

## 1. Intro: The Building Block of Life (0:00–0:40)

[Slow push from a person's hand into the skin, through tissue, until a single glowing cell fills the frame]

Your body is made of around thirty trillion cells. Every heartbeat, every thought, every breath you take starts here, in something so small that about ten could fit side by side across the width of a single hair.

[Old microscope, then a cork slice]

In 1665, Robert Hooke looked at a thin slice of cork and saw tiny boxes. He called them "cells". Nearly two centuries later, scientists realised that every living thing is made of cells, and that every cell comes from another cell.

[Camera dives toward the cell surface]

So what's actually going on inside one? Let's go in.

## 2. The Cell Membrane (0:40–1:40)

[The membrane fills the screen: a glowing surface of phospholipids]

First, we hit the wall: the cell membrane. It's only five to ten billionths of a metre thick, yet it decides what gets in and what stays out.

[Cutaway: two layers of molecules, heads out, tails in]

It's built from two layers of fatty molecules called phospholipids. Their heads love water and face outward. Their tails avoid water and hide inside. The result is a flexible, self-sealing barrier.

[Oxygen and carbon dioxide drift straight through]

Small molecules like oxygen and carbon dioxide slip right through. But most things need a door.

[Channel proteins open; ions stream through]

Proteins embedded in the membrane act as channels, gates and pumps.

[The sodium-potassium pump cycling]

This one is the sodium-potassium pump. For every molecule of ATP, the cell's energy currency, it spends, it pushes three sodium ions out and pulls two potassium ions in. A single cell can carry millions of these pumps, and together they use a big share of your body's energy at rest just to keep this balance.

[Signal molecules docking on receptors]

Other proteins are receptors, like antennas, that catch signals such as many hormones and pass the message inside.

## 3. Cytoplasm and the Cytoskeleton (1:40–2:20)

[We pass through the membrane into a crowded, glowing interior]

Inside, we're surrounded by cytoplasm: a crowded, jelly-like soup of water, salts and proteins, where all the cell's machinery floats.

[A network of filaments stretches across the cell]

Running through it is the cytoskeleton, a living scaffold of protein filaments that gives the cell its shape and lets it move.

[A motor protein "walks" along a microtubule carrying a vesicle]

And look at this. A motor protein called kinesin walks along a microtubule track, step by step, carrying a package of cargo across the cell. These deliveries happen constantly, all over the cell, in almost every cell of your body.

## 4. The Nucleus and DNA (2:20–3:30)

[A huge sphere appears: the nucleus, its surface covered in pores]

Ahead is the largest organelle in the cell: the nucleus, the control centre.

[Close on a nuclear pore, molecules passing through]

It's wrapped in a double membrane dotted with thousands of nuclear pores, guarded gateways that control what goes in and out.

[Inside: tangled glowing threads of chromatin]

Inside is your DNA. Stretched out, the DNA in just one cell would be about two metres long. To fit, it's wound around proteins called histones and coiled again and again into a material called chromatin.

[Zoom into the double helix]

DNA is a double helix: two strands holding hands through pairs of chemical letters, A with T and C with G. About three billion of these letter pairs, in a precise order, make up one copy of your genetic code, and most of your cells carry two copies: one from each parent.

[Highlighted section along the strand]

A stretch of DNA that carries the instructions for making a protein is called a gene. You have around twenty thousand of these protein-coding genes.

[A dense region inside the nucleus]

This darker region is the nucleolus. It's where the cell starts building its ribosomes, the machines we're about to meet.

## 5. From DNA to Protein (3:30–4:50)

[The helix unzips; an enzyme moves along it]

When the cell needs a protein, an enzyme called RNA polymerase unzips a small section of DNA and copies the gene into a messenger molecule: mRNA. This is called transcription.

[The mRNA is trimmed, then slides out through a nuclear pore]

The message is edited, then sent out through a nuclear pore into the cytoplasm.

[A ribosome clamps onto the mRNA]

There, a ribosome grabs it. The ribosome reads the message three letters at a time. Each three-letter "word" stands for one amino acid, or for "stop".

[tRNA molecules arriving, each carrying an amino acid]

Transfer RNA molecules bring the right amino acids, one by one, and the ribosome links them into a growing chain. This is translation.

[The chain lengthens and folds into a 3D shape]

As the chain grows, it folds into a precise 3D shape. That shape is everything: it decides whether the protein becomes an enzyme, a hormone, part of a muscle fibre or a door in the membrane.

[Many ribosomes working along one mRNA strand]

Several ribosomes can read the same message at once, so a single mRNA can make many copies of a protein.

## 6. The Factory Line: ER and Golgi (4:50–5:40)

[A vast folded membrane studded with ribosomes]

Many proteins are built on the endoplasmic reticulum, or ER: a maze of folded membranes connected to the nucleus. The rough ER is covered in ribosomes and feeds new proteins inside to finish folding.

[Smooth, tube-like section]

The smooth ER has no ribosomes. It makes fats, helps break down toxins, and stores calcium.

[Vesicles bud off and travel to a stack of flattened sacs]

Finished proteins are packed into bubbles called vesicles and sent to the Golgi apparatus, the cell's post office.

[Proteins tagged, sorted, new vesicles leaving]

The Golgi modifies them, adds address labels, and ships them off: to other parts of the cell, or out of the cell entirely.

[A vesicle fuses with the membrane and releases its cargo outside]

This is how your cells release things like hormones and digestive enzymes.

## 7. Power and Clean-Up (5:40–6:40)

[A glowing, bean-shaped mitochondrion; cutaway showing folded inner membranes]

All this work needs energy, and that comes from the mitochondria. They even have their own DNA, which you almost always inherit only from your mother.

[Inner membrane with rotating ATP synthase]

On their folded inner membranes, they use the food you eat and the oxygen you breathe to pump particles called protons. As the protons flow back, they spin a tiny rotating motor called ATP synthase, and every turn produces ATP.

[ATP molecules streaming out]

Your body makes and recycles roughly its own weight in ATP every single day.

[A lysosome engulfing a worn-out structure]

And every busy city needs clean-up crews. Lysosomes are sacs full of acid and digestive enzymes. They break down waste, swallowed bacteria, and even worn-out parts of the cell, recycling the pieces.

[Small round peroxisome]

Peroxisomes break down certain fatty acids and destroy hydrogen peroxide, a harmful by-product, before it can do damage.

## 8. Life Cycle and Finale (6:40–7:40)

[The chromatin condenses into X-shaped chromosomes]

Cells don't live forever. To grow and repair, many of them divide. First, the cell copies all of its DNA. The copies condense into chromosomes.

[Chromosomes line up, are pulled apart, and the cell pinches in two]

They line up in the middle, are pulled to opposite sides, and the cell pinches in two: two new cells, each with a complete copy of the instructions. This is cell division, and the splitting of the chromosomes is called mitosis. Your body makes millions of new cells every second.

[A neuron, a muscle cell, a red blood cell]

And not all cells look the same. Nerve cells can be over a metre long. Muscle cells contract. Red blood cells even push out their nucleus as they mature, leaving more room to carry oxygen. Your body has hundreds of cell types, all following the same basic plan.

[Pull back: one cell, a crowd of cells, tissue, skin, the person]

Trillions of cells, each one a busy city, working every second without you ever noticing.

[Title card]

You are not just made of cells. You are what they do together.

### Fact notes (checked)
- ~30 trillion human cells: Sender, Fuchs and Milo, 2016 (~30 × 10^12; earlier estimate 37 trillion, Bianconi 2013); Hatton et al., 2023: ~36 trillion in men and ~28 trillion in women. A typical cell is ~10 µm across; a hair is ~70–100 µm.
- Hooke, *Micrographia*, 1665. Cell theory: Schleiden and Schwann, 1838–39; "every cell from a cell": Virchow, 1855.
- Membrane ~5–10 nm thick. Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase: 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in per ATP; a large fraction of resting energy use.
- Kinesin walks along microtubules in ~8 nm steps.
- ~2 m of DNA per diploid cell; ~3.1 billion base pairs per haploid genome (~6.2 billion per diploid cell); ~20,000 protein-coding genes; a few thousand nuclear pores per nucleus; DNA is wrapped around histones.
- Ribosomal RNA is made and assembled in the nucleolus. Codons are 3 nucleotides; polysomes are many ribosomes on one mRNA.
- Mitochondrial DNA is maternally inherited (paternal transmission is extremely rare). ATP synthase is driven by the proton gradient set up by the electron transport chain, with oxygen as the final electron acceptor. Daily ATP turnover is roughly body weight (estimates 40–75 kg).
- Lysosome pH is ~4.5–5. Peroxisomes do β-oxidation and break down H₂O₂ with catalase.
- ~330 billion cells are replaced per day, about 3.8 million per second (Sender and Milo, 2021). Neurons can reach over 1 m (sciatic). Mature red blood cells have no nucleus.
